From push to pull

What if the factory only made things when someone asked?

Push

Every station works as fast as it can and shoves output to the next step, ready or not.

Result: piles of WIP. Remember SunCrest's 11.5 days?

Pull

The downstream step sends a signal upstream: "I just used some. Send me more."

Nothing is made until the signal arrives.

Kanban (Japanese for "signboard"): a visual signal, such as a card, an empty bin, or a screen, that authorizes production in a pull system.
The kanban loop · SunCrest Bakery

Baking ↔ Packaging

🔥 BAKINGupstream
full bins of loaves →
← empty bin + kanban card
📦 PACKAGINGdownstream
  1. Packaging uses up a bin of loaves.
  2. The empty bin goes back to Baking with its kanban card. That card is the order.
  3. Baking makes exactly one bin to refill it, and sends it forward.

No card, no production.

Try it in the room

Two people, a stack of paper, some sticky notes

A (upstream) folds a sheet. B (downstream) uses it. A may only fold when B hands back a sticky note: the card.

1 card

B finishes and has to wait for A to fold the next one.

B starves.

10 cards

A folds ahead; sheets pile up on B's desk.

We're back to push.

So how many cards is "just right"?
Two ways to run it

Single-card vs. two-card systems

Single-card

One card per container. The empty container + card go back upstream and trigger production. Simple; works when the two steps are close together.

Two-card

A withdrawal card moves material from the upstream stockpoint, and a production card tells the upstream step to replace it. Used when steps are farther apart.

Either way, the number of containers in the loop caps the WIP. That number is what we calculate.

The story

A coffee shop's custom printed cups

You order printed cups from a supplier. They arrive in plastic totes. When a tote is empty, you send it back and they refill it. That's a kanban loop.

dCups used per day500
vEmpty tote sits on the truck / dock0.1 days
rSupplier prints and fills the tote0.2 days
αCushion for flat tires and rush days0.10
cCups per tote50
v + r = 0.3 days is the replenishment lead time: how long you're "blind" between sending an empty tote and getting a full one back.
Solve it

k = 500 × (0.1 + 0.2) × (1 + 0.10) ÷ 50

① v + r = 0.1 + 0.2 = 0.3 days replenishment time
② d × (v + r) = 500 × 0.3 = 150 cups used while you wait
③ × (1 + α) = 150 × 1.10 = 165 cups plus the cushion
④ ÷ c = 165 ÷ 50 = 3.3 totes

k = 4 totes

Always round up. Three totes hold 150 cups, short of the 165 we need with the cushion.

What moves k?

Change one input at a time

What changesCalculationk
Supplier fills totes twice as fast (r = 0.1)500 × 0.2 × 1.1 ÷ 50 = 2.23
No safety cushion (α = 0)500 × 0.3 × 1.0 ÷ 50 = 3.03
Demand doubles (d = 1,000)1,000 × 0.3 × 1.1 ÷ 50 = 6.67

Faster replenishment means fewer containers, and less inventory. Lean attacks v + r rather than adding totes.

Takeaway

The kanban formula doesn't make you faster. It tells you exactly how much inventory covers the gap between using it and getting more.

  • It forces you to measure your lead time (v + r).
  • It makes you admit your uncertainty (α).
  • It sizes the buffer on purpose, instead of piling up stock "just in case."
